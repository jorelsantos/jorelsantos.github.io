Where Do UMSI Students Land?

8 years of career placement data, mined for what actually gets students hired.

Python · pandas · hypothesis testing · clustering

ContextUMSI's Career Development Office had 8 years of internship and full-time placement survey data (2017-2024) sitting in two separate, messy files — nobody had unified it to ask what actually predicts a good outcome. Team project with Tyler Tran and Kenny Wang.

What I didMerged and cleaned 1,374 placement records into one dataset, standardized organization names, and engineered a search-strategy category from free-text survey responses. Ran chi-square tests on career stage vs. strategy and on pre/post-COVID strategy shifts, a regression on strategy growth over time, and k-means/agglomerative clustering to find distinct placement profiles.

ResultsNetworking drove 25.7% of all placements — more than any other method, and the gap was statistically significant (χ²(5) = 361.25, p < 0.001). COVID didn't reverse that pattern, it accelerated it (χ²(5) = 29.80, p < 0.001): networking's share of placements grew after COVID, not despite it. A small number of organizations, led by the University of Michigan, Google, and Amazon, account for a disproportionate share of the pipeline.

What excites meTurning a messy institutional dataset into findings the Career Development Office could actually act on — different advice for internship vs. full-time seekers, and where to spend workshop time.

NBA Players Are Getting Leaner, Faster

A normalized SQL database of ~3,000 NBA players, built to test a hunch about how player builds are changing.

SQL · relational database design · DataGrip · ERD

ContextStarted with a raw dataset of roughly 3,000 NBA players and wanted to actually design a proper relational database around it, not just query a flat file — and use it to test whether player builds have shifted as the league has gotten more athletic.

What I didDesigned an ERD and built five normalized tables — players, schools, teams, positions, and countries — deliberately choosing weight and years of NBA experience as key fields over data like birthdate, and deliberately skipping a name/name_id lookup table where the data had no duplicates to justify one.

ResultsPlayers with fewer than 6 years of NBA experience average 206 lbs, versus 217 lbs for players with 6+ years — a real signal that incoming players trend leaner and faster than earlier cohorts. Separately, 317 of the ~3,000 players traced back to Big Ten schools.

What I learnedNormalization is a judgment call, not a default — deciding not to split out a table (no name/name_id table, since there were no duplicate names to protect against) was as much a design decision as deciding to split one out.

What excites meExtending this toward health and performance data across NBA eras — right now it captures build and experience, but the more interesting question is how player conditioning has evolved from college into the pros.
